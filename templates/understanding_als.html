Understanding ALS

What is ALS?

Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive neurodegenerative disease that affects nerve cells (motor neurons) in the brain and spinal cord. Also known as Lou Gehrig's disease or Motor Neuron Disease (MND), it causes the gradual death of neurons that control voluntary muscle movement.

In simple terms: Your brain sends messages through special nerve cells called motor neurons to tell your muscles what to do—like "move your hand" or "take a breath." In ALS, these motor neurons slowly break down and die. When they stop working, the messages can't get through, so muscles become weak and eventually stop moving.

Think of it like your home's electrical system:

  • Your brain is the main switch that sends the command "turn on the lights"
  • Motor neurons are the electrical wires that carry that command
  • Your muscles are the lights that turn on when they receive the signal

In ALS: The main switch (brain) works perfectly fine, but the wires (motor neurons) slowly get damaged. As each wire breaks down, that light (muscle) first flickers and dims, then eventually goes out. Different wires break at different times, which is why some muscles weaken before others.

Types of ALS

Sporadic ALS

Occurs randomly with no known family history. The cause is unknown but may involve genetic and environmental factors.

~90-95% of cases

Familial ALS

Inherited through genetic mutations (like C9orf72, SOD1). Children have a 50% chance of inheriting the gene.

~5-10% of cases

How ALS Affects the Body

Motor Neurons are specialized nerve cells that carry signals from the brain and spinal cord to the muscles, allowing us to perform voluntary movements like walking, speaking, and breathing.

Upper Motor Neurons (UMN)

Located in the motor cortex of the brain, they initiate voluntary movement and send signals down through the corticospinal tract to the spinal cord.

Lower Motor Neurons (LMN)

Located in the brainstem and spinal cord, they directly connect to muscles via the neuromuscular junction, triggering muscle contraction.

What Happens to Motor Neurons in ALS

  • Neurodegeneration: Motor neurons progressively weaken and die (apoptosis)
  • Axon Retraction: The nerve fibers connecting to muscles shrink and disconnect
  • Denervation: Muscles lose their nerve supply and cannot receive signals
  • Muscle Atrophy: Without stimulation, muscles weaken, shrink, and lose function
  • Progression: Damage typically spreads to adjacent motor neurons over time

Important: What ALS Does NOT Affect

  • Sensory Nerves: Sight, hearing, touch, taste, and smell remain fully intact
  • Cognitive Function: For most people, thinking and memory are preserved (though 35-50% may have mild changes)
  • Autonomic Functions: Bowel and bladder control are typically maintained
  • Eye Muscles: Extraocular muscles usually remain unaffected, even in late stages
  • Cardiac Muscle: The heart muscle continues to function normally

Common Signs and Symptoms

Because ALS damages the brain's connection to muscles, the symptoms are related to muscle weakness and control. It's important to remember that ALS is different for everyone; the symptoms and the order they appear in can vary greatly.

Limb-Onset

Starts in arms or legs (~70% of cases)

Bulbar-Onset

Starts with speech/swallowing (~25%)

Respiratory-Onset

Starts with breathing problems (~5%)

Muscle Weakness

Difficulty with tasks like buttoning shirts, writing, or climbing stairs

Muscle Twitching & Cramps

Small involuntary muscle twitches (fasciculations) and cramps

Speech & Swallowing Problems

Slurred speech (dysarthria) and difficulty swallowing (dysphagia)

Stiffness & Poor Coordination

Muscle tightness (spasticity) leading to awkward movements

Breathing Difficulties

Shortness of breath as respiratory muscles weaken

Progressive Movement Challenges

Increasing difficulty with walking and daily activities

Emotional Changes (PBA)

Pseudobulbar Affect: involuntary laughing or crying not matching emotions

Understanding Disease Progression

ALS is a progressive journey. While every path is unique, these are the general stages:

Initial Stage

• Muscle twitching (fasciculations)
• Subtle weakness in hands or feet
• Difficulty with fine motor tasks
• Tripping, dropping things
• Unexplained fatigue
• Slurred speech (if bulbar-onset)
• Diagnosis period (often 9-12 months)

Middle Stage

• Mobility aids needed
• Daily tasks harder
• Difficulty swallowing
• Breathing problems
• Changes in speech

Advanced Stage

• 24/7 ventilator support required
• tracheostomy needed
• Feeding tube required

Frequently Asked Questions

What causes ALS?
The exact cause of ALS is unknown in about 90% of cases (sporadic ALS). In the remaining 10% of cases, there is a known family history of the disease (familial ALS), linked to specific genetic mutations.
Is there a cure for ALS?
Currently, there is no cure for ALS. However, there are treatments, therapies, and medical devices that can help manage symptoms, slow the progression of the disease, and significantly improve a person's quality of life. Research is ongoing worldwide to find a cause and a cure, and new genetic therapies are showing promise for specific types of familial ALS.
Does ALS affect cognitive function?
ALS primarily affects motor function. However, about 35-50% of people with ALS experience some cognitive or behavioral changes. In approximately 10-15% of cases, ALS occurs with frontotemporal dementia (FTD), which can affect personality, behavior, and language. For the majority, thinking abilities, memory, and emotional capacity remain intact throughout the disease, allowing them to make decisions about their care.
How quickly does ALS progress?
ALS progression varies greatly from person to person. Some people may progress quickly over months, while others live with the disease for many years. The average survival time is 2-5 years after diagnosis, but about 20% of people live 5 years or longer, and some live 10+ years.

Remember

ALS changes how the body works, but it does not change who a person is. Every person with ALS deserves respect, good care, and a life filled with meaning.
